# Preparing Australia's Workforce for the Age of Artificial Intelligence

---

## 1. Problem Definition & Context

AI is not a prior automation technology. This is important.

AI is a supply shock to the labour market. Rather than cyclically reallocating tasks based on productivity levels, AI has started to supplant peoples' comparative advantages in task markets. Where a machinist once had tools that made her faster and more precise, now they have devices that can do her job entirely. This is the definition of structural unemployment.

The policy question is no longer if jobs will be lost to AI but who will lose those jobs and what will they do next.

**Channel 1: Task Displacement**

As the previous example highlights, AI is uniquely invasive across both manual and cognitive tasks. Because so many high-wage workers are concentrated in middle-skill cognitive jobs (Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 2023), displacement is likely to drive larger nominal falls in labour demand than past automation shocks. Recent evidence suggests up to one quarter of occupations have at least 70% of constituent tasks automatable with currently demonstrated tech (Goldman Sachs, 2023).

**Channel 2: Polarisation of Wages within Occupations**

Already we are seeing evidence that AI is driving a wedge between high- and low-experience workers within the same occupation. Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) provided workers with access to an otherwise identical generative AI tool and saw low-experience workers productivity increase by 34% while high-experience workers were almost unaffected. Displaced demographics will likely see less bargaining power when switching occupations due to these compressed experience premiums.

**Channel 3: Growth of "Superstar" Firms**

The last channel by which AI might contribute to weaker labour demand is concentrated productivity growth in capital-intensive firms. While record profits currently enjoyed by multinationals are nothing new, AI systems allow them to maintain or grow these profits while hiring far fewer employees. Exposure to AI has been shown to increase the labour share of income elasticity by -13% (Autor et al., 2020). As superstar firms grow, but employees cannot, aggregate labour demand suffers.

As with any labour market disruption, AI will create jobs as it destroys them. Unlike prior disruptions, AI is hitting jobs at the top of the value chain, where workers have both enjoyed higher wages and greater reemployment opportunities (based on employment data from Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 2023). Not acting now to alleviate the pain these workers will face will have significant consequences for public buy-in of AI down the line.

---

## 2. Analysis of Impacts

Employment impacts will not be evenly distributed but concentrated in occupations with high AI exposure. Already we are seeing sectoral employment growth decouple from professional layoffs in AI exposed industries. Legal occupations, for example, have seen increased workloads with no corresponding growth in lawyer employment (Mckeown, 2023). In absolute terms employment effects are uncertain, but for groups overrepresented in high AI exposure sectors — women, workers without tertiary credentials, and First Nations workers — job loss is a real possibility. The growth industries that will create jobs in the age of AI — medicine, care work, green construction — are intensive in either skills or labour that displaced workers simply do not have.

## 3. Policy Options

**Option One: National AI Reskilling Fund**

Option One is a $2 billion market response fund that would support partnered training providers with upskilling, reskilling, and allowance payments to unemployed workers. Funded by an uplift to the Gas Windfall Tax and focused on occupational clusters identified by industry as having jobs to fill, Option One represents a direct but costly way to mitigate harm to displaced workers by supporting transitions to growth sectors. Risks of adopting Option One include: waterbed effects if labour demand doesn't keep up with industry needs, a never fully absorbed fund that draws on government revenues, and public backlash if money is perceived to be "given" to incumbent workers.

**Option Two: Regulatory Safeguards**

Option Two emphasizes effective deployment of AI over distribution of recovery funds. Minimum wages for blue-collar labour, safety standards, tax audits etc. all have one thing in common; they are designed to stop businesses/managers/operators from engaging in specific activities. Option Two does not put a moratorium on automation, but neither does it do anything to cushion workers from initial shocks. Concerns about underinvestment in worker protections are valid, but that is the choice presented: accept exposure to harm in exchange for giving employers the tools to grow.

**Option Three: Portable Entitlements**

Think truck drivers have a hard time switching careers? Australia's industrial relations system may make it harder still. Accumulating two weeks of paid leave over two years, having skill-specific certificates or 'points' that may not transfer across occupations are just some of the friction workers face when changing jobs. Option Three covers adapting the MUTCD to ensure that when workers do leave AI exposed industries they're ready to do so. Potential downsides to portability are limited but may disincentivise workers from staying, task-forcing specific skills.

Of the three options above, a hybrid of Option One and Option Two is recommended. Neither do enough on their own to protect vulnerable workers but together support the dual imperatives of minimizing damage and rewarding workers who undertake reskilling efforts. Establishing a transition fund in 2026 with the guardrails of Option Two established immediately would hedge our bets against uncertainty while maintaining AI adaptation as a net-positive.

---

## 4. Recommended Package & Implementation Plan

Our analysis favours the Option One + Option Two package. Option Two provides immediate, low-cost structural protection where sectors are regulated. Option One provides the medium-term reskilling capability required for sustained labour market transition. Option Three could be considered for inclusion in a follow-on tranche of legislation (2028–2030) once the initial measures have bedded down and can be reviewed.

| Stage | Timeframe | Key Actions | Responsibilities | KPIs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Foundation** | Q1–Q4 2026 | Introduce AI Transition Fund; commence industry co-design councils in 6 target sectors; publish AI exposure data by occupation & location | DEWR, Skills Australia | 40k training commencements; industry councils established |
| **2. Regulation** | Q2 2026–Q2 2027 | Publish Tier 1 AI safety taxonomy; publish SME compliance guide; introduce mandatory human-in-the-loop regulation with 1-year transition period | DISR, Treasury | 90% compliance for Tier 1; SME toolkit adoption > 60% |
| **3. Scale & Evaluate** | 2027–2029 | Upscale micro-credential pathways proven effective; independent assessment of outcomes data for new employment; draft legislation for portable entitlements | DEWR, Fair Work Commission | Rate of new employment for displaced workers > 70%; temporary wage floor maintained for Tier 1 sectors |
| **4. Consolidation** | 2029–2030 | Final rollout of portable entitlements; cross-agency policy review with KPIs; benchmark against international AI regulation frameworks | Treasury, all agencies | Labour share stabilised; reduce equity gap between demographics |

**Estimated cost:** $2.4 billion over five years, partially offset by reduced income support expenditure and a productivity dividend estimated at $1.1 billion (Productivity Commission, 2024).

## 5. Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skills training not aligned with employer needs | Medium | High | Mandatory industry/workforce representative co-design & investment, Jobs and Skills Australia-led dashboard of real-time data on occupation demands |
| Regulatory arbitrage (AI decisions offshored) | Medium | Medium | Include 'jurisdiction of effect' provisions in legislation; coordinate with like-minded countries on AI governance, such as the G7 |
| Small & medium enterprise (SME) burden of compliance weakens Option Two | High | Medium | Tier penalties by firm size; offer free access to compliance toolkit; introduce grace period of 12 months |
| Regional Australians miss out on reskilling | Medium | High | Emphasise online-first delivery of training; introduce regional navigators to provide support & information; allow for travel and relocation expenses as part of training packages |
| Political resistance delays legislation | High | Medium | Set up as a cross-party Senate inquiry; initiate pilot programs to show proof of concept early |
